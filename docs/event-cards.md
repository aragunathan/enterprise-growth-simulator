# Enterprise Growth Simulator — Event Cards (Phase 2 draft, enterprise-scale revision)

**Company**: a Dubai-headquartered, multi-business-unit enterprise — roughly 4,200 employees and ~$120M revenue at Q1, growing to ~7,000 employees and ~$250-300M revenue by Q8. Revised to enterprise scale to match the actual roundtable audience ($100M+ revenue organizations) — the story now opens at a scale those leaders currently recognize as their own, not one they left behind years ago.
**KPI effect notation**: directional only (small / medium / large, up or down). Exact point values are a later balancing pass, done after a first read-through/playtest — not guessed here.
**Live headline KPIs**: Revenue Growth, Decision Velocity, Enterprise Friction. All others surface only in the closing report.

---

## Q1 — A regional demand surge outpaces approvals
**Headcount: ~4,200**

**Business context**: A regional demand surge is pushing deal volume up sharply, but the approval chain — built for the company's current three business units — wasn't designed for this velocity. Contracts, vendor onboarding, and hiring requests are stacking up in committees never meant to move this fast.

**Why it happens**: UAE real estate and infrastructure momentum is pulling enterprise pipelines up fast across sectors. This isn't a lack of process — it's a process architecture calibrated for steady-state growth, buckling under a compressed timeline.

**Choices**:
- **A.** Stand up a dedicated cross-functional deal-acceleration unit to own the surge
- **B.** Temporarily raise self-approval thresholds for business unit heads, formalize governance later
- **C.** Triage: fast-track top-tier strategic accounts, slow-walk the rest
- **D.** Bring in a specialist external surge team to clear the backlog without changing internal process

**Immediate consequences**: A → Enterprise Friction ↓small, Cash ↓medium, Decision Velocity ↑medium · B → Decision Velocity ↑large, Compliance ↓small, Enterprise Friction ↑small (untracked risk) · C → Revenue Growth ↓small (deprioritized accounts), Enterprise Friction ↓medium · D → Cash ↓medium, Revenue Growth ↑small (backlog cleared fast), Enterprise Friction ↑small (no structural fix, debt persists)

**Delayed effect → Q2 (1 quarter later)**: this choice sets the documentation baseline the government tender depends on. A/C leave a clean paper trail; B/D leave gaps (B from informal approvals, D from an external team unfamiliar with internal governance) that surface as a scramble in Q2.

**KPIs affected**: Revenue Growth, Decision Velocity, Enterprise Friction, Cash, Compliance

## Q2 — The AED 50M government tender
**Headcount: ~4,300**

**Business context**: A landmark AED 50 million government tender opens — the kind of flagship win that would meaningfully expand the company's public-sector footprint. But governance and compliance records live across five different business-unit systems, inconsistent in format and worse if Q1 leaned toward speed over process.

**Why it happens**: The UAE's active public-sector infrastructure pipeline routinely produces opportunities exactly this size. At enterprise scale, the challenge isn't "we've never done this" — it's that the company's own compliance history is scattered across units that don't share a system.

**Choices**:
- **A.** Hire external consultants/legal to assemble a compliant bid fast
- **B.** Delay submission a cycle to build proper internal documentation
- **C.** Unify governance and compliance records across business units, submit only when ready
- **D.** Submit a narrower bid, covering only the business unit(s) with clean documentation

**Immediate consequences**: A → Cash ↓medium, Compliance ↑medium, Revenue Growth ↑medium · B → Revenue Growth ↓large (miss the cycle), Compliance ↑medium · C → Decision Velocity ↓medium (near-term), Compliance ↑large · D → Revenue Growth ↑small (smaller win secured), Compliance ↑medium (clean within scope), Enterprise Friction ↓small (avoided overreach)

**Delayed effect → Q5 (3 quarters later)**: how much governance ground got covered now shapes how hard the e-invoicing rollout is. A/C cover the most ground and ease Q5 the most; D leaves some business units still undocumented, a moderate drag on Q5; B does no governance work at all and leaves Q5 hardest.

**KPIs affected**: Revenue Growth, Cash, Compliance, Decision Velocity, Enterprise Friction

## Q3 — The Saudi Arabia ask
**Headcount: ~4,600**

**Business context**: A flagship customer relationship — large enough to matter materially to revenue — asks the company to establish and deliver operations in Saudi Arabia. Despite the company's scale domestically, it has zero KSA presence, and real cross-border friction applies: separate licensing (MISA), minimum capital requirements, and a corporate tax rate on foreign entities that doesn't apply back home.

**Why it happens**: Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030-driven expansion is pulling large UAE enterprises across the border to follow anchor clients — but KSA market entry is a genuinely different regulatory regime, not an extension of existing UAE operations, regardless of how large the parent company already is.

**Choices**:
- **A.** Establish a full licensed Saudi entity — heavy upfront investment
- **B.** Serve the client remotely from the UAE, informally, no local entity
- **C.** Partner or joint-venture with an existing Saudi-licensed firm
- **D.** Decline the expansion, stay UAE-only, risk losing the client

**Immediate consequences**: A → Cash ↓large, Revenue Growth ↑large (long-run), Enterprise Friction ↑medium · B → Revenue Growth ↑small, Compliance ↓medium (regulatory grey zone) · C → Cash ↓small, Revenue Growth ↑medium, Decision Velocity ↓small · D → Revenue Growth ↓medium, Cash preserved, Enterprise Friction ↓small

**Delayed effect → Q7 (4 quarters later)**: how cleanly cross-border operations were set up now determines how badly M&A integration compounds later. A/C give a clean structure; B leaves hidden liabilities; D removes this thread entirely.

**KPIs affected**: Revenue Growth, Cash, Compliance, Enterprise Friction, Decision Velocity

## Q4 — The board's AI mandate
**Headcount: ~4,900**

**Business context**: The board demands a visible, enterprise-wide AI strategy — competitors are moving, investors are asking. Years of organic growth and prior acquisitions have left data fragmented across legacy business-unit systems, with no shared governance layer underneath any of it.

**Why it happens**: Board and investor pressure on visible AI results is intense across UAE enterprises right now, but the confidence large organizations have in their own AI governance frequently outpaces what they could actually defend to a regulator — a legacy-scale data fragmentation problem, not a "we never built anything" problem.

**Choices**:
- **A.** Invest in an enterprise-wide data governance foundation first, delay visible pilots
- **B.** Launch a flashy customer-facing AI pilot now, for the board
- **C.** Bring in an external consultancy to fast-track foundation and pilot together
- **D.** Let each business unit adopt AI tools independently, no central strategy

**Immediate consequences**: A → Innovation ↓small (near-term), Compliance ↑medium, Enterprise Friction ↓small · B → Innovation ↑large, Compliance ↓medium, Enterprise Friction ↑medium, Revenue Growth ↑small (board-pleasing narrative) · C → Cash ↓large, Innovation ↑medium, Compliance ↑medium, Revenue Growth ↑small · D → Innovation ↑medium, Decision Velocity ↑small (locally), Enterprise Friction ↑large (fragmentation across units), Revenue Growth ↓small (inconsistent AI story undermines large clients)

**Delayed effect → Q6 (2 quarters later)**: skipped governance now (B/D) means the cyber incident hits harder — sprawling, ungoverned tools across business units are extra attack surface. A/C soften that blow.

**KPIs affected**: Innovation, Compliance, Cash, Enterprise Friction, Decision Velocity, Revenue Growth

## Q5 — E-invoicing becomes mandatory
**Headcount: ~5,200**

**Business context**: The UAE's e-invoicing mandate becomes binding for the company — well above the revenue threshold that triggers the earliest compliance deadline. Finance, procurement, ERP, tax, and contracts across every business unit must connect to a certified structured invoicing system on a hard regulatory date.

**Why it happens**: This is a live regulatory clock, not a hypothetical — a phased mandate with a pilot phase and hard mandatory dates already set by the Federal Tax Authority, and at this revenue scale the company is squarely in the first wave required to comply.

**Choices**:
- **A.** Appoint an accredited service provider, rebuild the workflow properly across all business units
- **B.** Bolt on the minimum technical compliance layer, no workflow redesign
- **C.** Bring in a specialist implementation partner to lead the transition
- **D.** Roll out full compliance to the legally in-scope business units now, stage the rest over the following year

**Immediate consequences**: A → Cash ↓medium, Compliance ↑large, Decision Velocity ↓small (near-term), Revenue Growth ↑small (smooth B2G/enterprise transactions) · B → Cash ↓small, Compliance ↑small, Enterprise Friction ↑medium (fragile patchwork) · C → Cash ↓large, Compliance ↑large, Decision Velocity ↑small · D → Cash ↓small, Compliance ↑medium (compliant where legally required), Enterprise Friction ↑small (deferred units are a known, managed gap, not a reckless one), Revenue Growth ↑small (protects B2G eligibility for the mandatory scope)

**Delayed effect**: this quarter is itself a receiver (from Q2) — no further scripted forward link, but its outcome feeds Enterprise Friction generally into the back half of the game.

**KPIs affected**: Cash, Compliance, Decision Velocity, Enterprise Friction, Revenue Growth

## Q6 — Cyber incident
**Headcount: ~5,400**

**Business context**: A security incident is detected, touching systems shared across multiple business units. IT freezes all new deployments company-wide to contain it, halting several in-flight projects.

**Why it happens**: Rapid, multi-unit scaling plus possibly ungoverned AI/tooling sprawl from Q4 widens the attack surface considerably at this size — a pattern many large, fast-scaling GCC enterprises are currently facing as adoption outpaces security architecture.

**Choices**:
- **A.** Full freeze until root cause is confirmed and fixed — security first
- **B.** Targeted freeze on affected systems only, keep the rest moving
- **C.** Continue deployments, patch in parallel — accept short-term risk
- **D.** Bring in an external incident response firm to compress the freeze window

**Immediate consequences**: A → Innovation ↓large, Compliance ↑medium, Enterprise Friction ↓small (contained), Revenue Growth protected (no visible incident) · B → Innovation ↓medium, Decision Velocity ↑small, Enterprise Friction ↑small (partial exposure) · C → Innovation ↑small, Compliance ↓large, Enterprise Friction ↑large, Revenue Growth ↓medium (breach becomes visible, client trust drops) · D → Cash ↓large, Compliance ↑medium, Enterprise Friction ↓medium, Revenue Growth protected

**Delayed effect → Q8 (2 quarters later)**: how the incident was handled sets the crisis-response muscle the org has when the next shock hits — A/D build a practiced, fast-moving response capability; C leaves the org still improvising under pressure.

**KPIs affected**: Innovation, Compliance, Cash, Enterprise Friction, Decision Velocity, Revenue Growth

## Q7 — The acquisition
**Headcount: ~5,400 pre-merger, ~6,800 post-merger**

**Business context**: The company acquires a sizable competitor to consolidate market position and secure talent. Now there are two ERPs, two HR systems, different procurement policies, duplicate vendors, and conflicting approval hierarchies — at a scale where "just merge the spreadsheets" was never an option.

**Why it happens**: Consolidation is a common pattern for large, fast-growing UAE enterprises once capital is available and yesterday's competitors become today's acquisition targets.

**Choices**:
- **A.** Integrate everything immediately — one ERP, one HR system, one policy set
- **B.** Run both systems in parallel for a year, integrate slowly
- **C.** Keep the acquired company semi-autonomous, integrate only finance/compliance
- **D.** Integrate people and culture first, defer systems integration entirely

**Immediate consequences**: A → Cash ↓large, Enterprise Friction ↓large after a rough transition, Decision Velocity ↑large, Employee Experience ↓medium, Revenue Growth ↑medium (synergies realized) · B → Cash ↓small, Enterprise Friction ↑large (ongoing duplication), Decision Velocity ↓medium, Revenue Growth ↓small (delivery disrupted) · C → Cash ↓medium, Compliance ↑medium, Enterprise Friction ↑medium, Revenue Growth ↑small · D → Employee Experience ↑medium, Enterprise Friction ↑large (systems chaos persists), Decision Velocity ↓large, Revenue Growth ↓small (delivery disrupted)

**Delayed effect → Q8 (1 quarter later)**: whatever friction level exists post-merger carries straight into the finale. Clean integration (A/C) gives the org real agility to respond fast when the shock hits; B/D mean it enters Q8 still tangled and slower to react.

**KPIs affected**: Cash, Enterprise Friction, Decision Velocity, Employee Experience, Compliance, Revenue Growth

## Q8 — Sudden regional disruption
**Headcount: ~7,000**

**Business context**: Overnight, a regional security disruption closes off a key shipping route the company depends on for materials and client deliveries. Freight costs and insurance premiums for regional operations spike immediately. A major client relationship — the kind that matters materially to revenue — depends on reliable, on-time delivery through exactly this period.

**Why it happens**: Operating in this region carries this kind of macro shock as a recurring, if unpredictable, operational risk — the sort of disruption every enterprise here has to have a real answer for, whether or not it's ever actually hit them.

**Choices**:
- **A.** Absorb the cost — pay premium freight/insurance to hold service levels for the client
- **B.** Renegotiate scope and timeline with the client, buy time to find alternatives
- **C.** Rapidly diversify to emergency alternative suppliers/routes — real cost, builds lasting resilience
- **D.** Bring in a specialized logistics/risk partner to manage the disruption on a shared-risk contract

**Immediate consequences**: A → Cash ↓large, Customer Experience ↑large, Revenue Growth protected · B → Customer Experience ↓medium, Cash preserved, Revenue Growth ↓small (reduced scope) · C → Cash ↓medium, Enterprise Friction ↓small (new resilience built), Customer Experience ↑small, Revenue Growth ↑small (long-term positioning) · D → Cash ↓medium, Customer Experience ↑medium, Revenue Growth protected, Enterprise Friction ↓small (risk shared with a specialist, not carried internally)

**Delayed effect**: none — this is the finale. How well the org handles a shock nobody chose resolves directly into the closing executive report, shaped by the crisis-response muscle built in Q6 and the organizational agility (or chaos) left over from Q7.

**KPIs affected**: Cash, Customer Experience, Revenue Growth, Enterprise Friction
